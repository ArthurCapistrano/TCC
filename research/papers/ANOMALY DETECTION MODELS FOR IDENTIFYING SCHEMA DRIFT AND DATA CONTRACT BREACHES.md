Introduction** The rapid growth of data-intensive applications has led organizations to depend heavily on automated data pipelines for analytics, decision-making, and operational intelligence. These pipelines typically integrate data from multiple heterogeneous sources, each evolving independently over time. As a result, **schema drift** —defined as unexpected changes in data structure, data types, or field semantics—has emerged as a major challenge to maintaining consistent data quality and system reliability. Closely related to this challenge is the increasing adoption of **data contracts**, which formally specify expectations between data producers and consumers regarding schema, freshness, completeness, and validity. Violations of these contracts can severely disrupt downstream analytics and decision processes.

While effective in controlled environments, these approaches struggle to cope with dynamic data ecosystems where changes occur frequently and often unpredictably. In contrast, anomaly detection models offer adaptive capabilities that allow systems to learn normal patterns of data behavior and identify deviations that may indicate **schema drift** or data contract breaches. Recent advances in machine learning have demonstrated strong potential for detecting subtle anomalies in complex, high-dimensional signals, making them particularly suitable for modern data pipelines (Alnegheimish et al., 2022; Ihsan & Astuti, 2022). In parallel, automated ETL pipeline models have emphasized the importance of embedding data quality checks and governance controls directly into pipeline workflows to ensure continuous compliance (Ogunsola et al., 2022). Moreover, the challenge of **schema drift** can be viewed as a specific instance of concept drift in evolving data streams, where the underlying data distribution changes over time. Research on cost-sensitive learning and drift-aware models highlights the need for adaptive detection strategies that balance detection accuracy with operational efficiency (Sun et al., 2021). This section categorizes and discusses the principal anomaly detection approaches applicable to detecting such violations, drawing on insights from prior machine learning and data quality research. **3.1 Statistical and Rule-Augmented Anomaly Detection** Statistical approaches represent one of the earliest methods for anomaly detection in data systems. These techniques rely on historical baselines of schema metadata and data distributions, such as mean, variance, frequency of null values, or categorical cardinality. Deviations beyond predefined thresholds are flagged as potential anomalies. While simple and computationally efficient, purely statistical methods are limited in their ability to detect complex, non-linear schema changes. However, when augmented with rule-based constraints derived from **data** **contracts**, they offer a practical first line of defense in automated ETL pipelines (Ogunsola et al., 2022). **3.2 Machine Learning–Based Anomaly Detection** Machine learning–based approaches learn normal patterns of data and schema behavior from historical observations and identify anomalies as deviations from these learned patterns. Unsupervised models such as isolation forests, clustering-based methods, and autoencoders are particularly suitable when labeled anomaly data is scarce. Frameworks like Sintel demonstrate how multivariate signal learning can be leveraged to capture subtle correlations across features, making these models effective for detecting **schema drift** that may not violate explicit rules but still disrupt downstream analytics (Alnegheimish et al., 2022). **3.3 Deep Learning Approaches** Deep learning models extend traditional machine learning by capturing complex temporal and structural dependencies in data. Recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and deep autoencoders have shown strong performance in anomaly detection tasks involving high-dimensional and sequential data. Evidence from industrial monitoring applications indicates that deep learning–based anomaly detectors are effective in environments characterized by noisy data and evolving patterns (Ihsan & Astuti, 2022). These capabilities make them well suited for detecting gradual **schema drift** and recurring data contract violations over time. **3.4 Drift-Aware and Cost-Sensitive Models** data change over time. Drift-aware models dynamically update their learned representations to adapt to new data distributions. Cost-sensitive learning techniques further enhance these models by assigning different penalties to false positives and false negatives, which is particularly important in data governance contexts where undetected contract breaches may have severe downstream consequences (Sun et al., 2021).

**3.5 Integration with Data Contract Governance** Beyond detection accuracy, anomaly detection models must align with organizational governance and contract enforcement strategies. Predictive models for data contract failures enable proactive interventions before violations propagate across systems (Chirumamilla, 2023). Additionally, governance-oriented perspectives emphasize the importance of transparency, accountability, and automated control mechanisms in enforcing contracts across digital platforms (Yang et al., 2022). Integrating anomaly detection outputs with versioned data architectures further enhances traceability and auditability (Alluri, 2021). **4\. Proposed Anomaly Detection Framework for **Schema Drift** and Data Contract Breaches** To systematically address **schema drift** and data contract violations, this study proposes a layered anomaly detection framework integrated within modern data pipeline architectures. The framework combines metadata monitoring, statistical profiling, and machine learning–based anomaly detection to enable continuous and proactive enforcement of **data contracts**. The framework begins at the data ingestion layer, where raw data and schema metadata are collected from diverse sources. Schema attributes such as field names, data types, null ratios, and cardinality are continuously logged and versioned using auditable storage mechanisms. This historical metadata forms the baseline for identifying structural changes over time (Alluri, 2021). At the feature extraction and monitoring layer, schema-level and data-level features are derived, including distributional statistics, schema evolution indicators, and contract compliance metrics. These features are then passed to the anomaly detection layer, which hosts multiple detection models operating in parallel. Statistical detectors provide fast checks against known constraints, while machine learning and deep learning models capture subtle, non-linear deviations that may signal gradual **schema drift** or emerging contract breaches (Alnegheimish et al., 2022; Ihsan & Astuti, 2022). Detected anomalies are evaluated against predefined **data contracts** to determine severity and impact. When violations are confirmed, the response and governance layer triggers alerts, automated remediation actions, or rollback mechanisms. This aligns anomaly detection outcomes with organizational governance and contract control strategies, ensuring accountability and transparency (Yang et al., 2022; Chirumamilla, 2023).

**5.1 Evaluation Metrics** Commonly used metrics include precision, recall, and F1-score, which measure the correctness of detected anomalies against known schema changes or documented contract violations. High recall is particularly important in governance-sensitive environments, as undetected **schema drift** can propagate errors to downstream analytics. However, excessive false positives can erode trust in monitoring systems, making precision equally critical. In streaming and near–real-time pipelines, detection latency is an additional key metric. Early identification of **schema drift** allows corrective actions—such as schema reconciliation or rollback—to be applied before data quality degradation spreads. Cost-aware evaluation strategies are also relevant, as different types of contract breaches may carry different business risks (Sun et al., 2021). **5.2 Use-Case Scenarios** One primary use case involves **schema drift** detection in evolving data sources, where new attributes are introduced or existing fields change data types without prior notification. Anomaly detection models identify these deviations by comparing incoming metadata against learned historical patterns, enabling rapid response supported by versioned storage layers (Alluri, 2021). A second use case focuses on data contract breach identification, where violations such as missing mandatory fields, abnormal null ratios, or delayed data arrivals are detected. Predictive models trained on historical contract failures can anticipate breaches before they fully manifest, supporting proactive remediation strategies (Chirumamilla, 2023). A third scenario addresses concept drift in long-running pipelines, where gradual changes in data distributions may not immediately violate explicit contracts but still affect analytical validity. Drift-aware anomaly detection models adapt over time while maintaining governance visibility through continuous monitoring (Alnegheimish et al., 2022). **Description:** The figure illustrates how input data streams and schema metadata feed into anomaly detection

Challenges and Future Research Directions** Despite the growing effectiveness of anomaly detection models for identifying **schema drift** and data contract breaches, several challenges remain that limit their widespread adoption and operational robustness in real-world data environments. One major challenge is the scarcity of labeled anomaly data. **Schema drift** and contract violations often occur infrequently or in unpredictable forms, making supervised learning approaches difficult to train reliably. As a result, most existing solutions rely on unsupervised or semi- supervised methods, which may struggle to distinguish between benign changes and harmful anomalies. Future research should explore hybrid learning strategies that combine limited labeled data with unsupervised detection to improve interpretability and accuracy (Alnegheimish et al., 2022). Another challenge relates to the dynamic nature of data ecosystems. Frequent schema evolution and changing business requirements can cause detection models to become outdated, leading to model drift. While drift-aware and adaptive learning approaches address this issue to some extent, maintaining stability without excessive retraining remains an open problem. Research on cost- sensitive and incremental learning techniques offers promising directions for balancing adaptability and computational efficiency (Sun et al., 2021). False positives and alert fatigue also pose significant operational challenges. Overly sensitive anomaly detection systems may generate excessive alerts, reducing trust among data engineers and governance teams. Future systems should incorporate context-aware detection and severity ranking mechanisms to prioritize anomalies that are most likely to result in critical data contract breaches (Chirumamilla, 2023). From a governance perspective, aligning technical anomaly detection outputs with organizational control and accountability structures remains complex. Effective enforcement of **data contracts** requires not only accurate detection but also transparent reporting, traceability, and auditability. Integrating anomaly detection with versioned storage and governance frameworks can enhance trust and compliance, but standardized practices are still evolving (Alluri, 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Effective detection of **schema drift** and data contract breaches is essential for maintaining data quality, reliability, and trust in modern analytics pipelines. This study examined the role of anomaly detection models—ranging from statistical approaches to machine learning and deep learning methods—in identifying deviations in schema structure, data distributions, and contract compliance. By integrating anomaly detection with versioned data architectures and automated ETL workflows, organizations can achieve proactive monitoring, timely remediation, and enhanced governance of their data assets (Alluri, 2021; Ogunsola et al., 2022). The proposed framework highlights a multi-layered approach, combining metadata monitoring, feature extraction, and adaptive detection models, with outputs mapped to contract enforcement mechanisms and organizational governance structures. Evaluation metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and detection latency provide quantitative measures to assess model effectiveness, while use-case scenarios demonstrate practical applications in **schema drift** detection, contract breach identification, and concept drift adaptation (Chirumamilla, 2023; Sun et al., 2021). Despite these advances, challenges remain in handling limited labeled data, dynamic pipeline environments, false positives, and alignment with governance policies. Future research should focus on explainable models, adaptive learning strategies, and standardized evaluation frameworks to further enhance anomaly detection systems in complex, evolving data ecosystems.
